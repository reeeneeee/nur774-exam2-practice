Q1. [Single] An 8-month-old has had dry, rough, red patches on the cheeks and the outer surfaces of the arms since 3 months of age. The parent says the infant rubs her face against the crib sheets constantly, especially in the evening, and that the patches clear for a while and then come back. The father has asthma and allergic rhinitis. Which diagnosis and phase best fit this presentation?

A. Seborrheic dermatitis (cradle cap), which usually resolves in the first year

B. Atopic dermatitis, childhood phase

C. Atopic dermatitis, infantile phase

D. Irritant contact dermatitis from repeated exposure to saliva

Show answer

Answer: C

Rationale: Severe itching that gets worse in the evening, dry skin, flares followed by remission, and a strong family history of atopy point to atopic dermatitis. Onset at 3 months puts it in the infantile phase (onset 1–6 months, can continue to 2 years). Seborrheic dermatitis does not itch and favors hair-bearing intertriginous areas. The childhood phase begins between 2 years and puberty.

Q2. [SATA] A 2-month-old has a red, scaly rash. The nurse practitioner is trying to tell atopic dermatitis apart from seborrheic dermatitis. Which findings favor atopic dermatitis? Select all that apply.

A. The infant is fussy and rubs the affected areas constantly, and it is worse in the evening

B. A symmetrical, red, scaling eruption limited to the scalp, behind the ears, and the skin folds, with no itching

C. The mother has eczema and a known elevated IgE level

D. The patches flare, clear completely, and then flare again

E. The eruption is attributed to overproduction of sebum with Malassezia yeast

F. Dry, rough skin with red papules on the cheeks

Show answer

Answer: A, C, D, F

Rationale: Atopic dermatitis is "the itch that rashes." It causes severe itching that worsens in the evening, abnormally dry skin with red papules, periods of flare and remission, and is linked to elevated IgE with a strong family history. A non-itchy, symmetrical, scaling eruption in hair-bearing intertriginous areas, caused by sebum overproduction and Malassezia, describes seborrheic dermatitis.

Answer: D

Rationale: Lesions that form at a break in the skin, such as eczema, are secondary impetigo. The blueprint states that bullous and secondary impetigo are almost always caused by S. aureus. Answer: D

Rationale: Vesicles that become bullae and rupture into a thin, varnish-like scale are bullous impetigo, which is almost always caused by S. aureus. With multiple lesions, the blueprint calls for an oral antibiotic active against beta-lactamase-producing S. aureus for 7–10 days, and dicloxacillin is the professor's preferred choice. Q99. [SATA] Which rash descriptions are correctly matched with the most likely diagnosis? Select all that apply.

A. Greasy scale on the scalp, eyebrows, and nasolabial folds of a well infant: seborrheic dermatitis

B. A rash only where a wristwatch band sits, which appeared after the child started wearing the new watch: allergic contact dermatitis

C. Itchy vesicles in different stages, concentrated on the trunk and scalp, in an unvaccinated child: varicella

D. Itchy, lichenified flexural plaques in a 4-year-old with asthma and a strong family history of atopy: seborrheic dermatitis

E. A red macule surrounded by a clear ring and then a larger red outer ring at the site of a tick bite: erythema migrans

F. Sandpaper-textured skin with circumoral pallor and a strawberry tongue: measles

Show answer

Answer: A, B, C, E

Rationale: Greasy scale in hair-bearing areas is seborrheic dermatitis. A rash confined to where a new item touches the skin is allergic contact dermatitis. Itchy, centripetal vesicles in different stages are varicella, and a bull's-eye lesion at a tick bite is erythema migrans. Lichenified, itchy plaques with a strong atopic history are atopic dermatitis, not seborrheic dermatitis. Sandpaper skin with a strawberry tongue and circumoral pallor is scarlet fever, not measles.

Q100. [Single] An unvaccinated 17-year-old who smokes is on day 3 of varicella. He now has cough, shortness of breath, tachypnea, and an oxygen saturation of 90%. Which management is most appropriate?

A. Oral acyclovir, because he is still within the 72-hour treatment window

B. Supportive care with scheduled ibuprofen and antihistamines, because varicella is benign in healthy adolescents

C. An antistaphylococcal penicillin for pyoderma

D. IV acyclovir for severe disease (varicella pneumonia), recognizing smoking as a risk factor

Show answer

Answer: D

Rationale: New respiratory distress and hypoxia in a smoker with varicella suggest varicella pneumonia, which is severe disease. The blueprint says IV acyclovir is effective for severe disease and for immunocompromised patients. The oral acyclovir window in the blueprint is 24 hours, and oral treatment is not appropriate for pneumonia.
