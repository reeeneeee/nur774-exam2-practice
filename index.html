Q1. [Single] A mother brings her 7-year-old son for a well visit. Answer: B

Rationale: The childhood phase starts between 2 years and puberty, and this child's onset was at age 3. The infantile phase starts at 1–6 months and can continue up to 2 years. The adult phase starts after 12 years. Option D gets the infantile age range wrong.

Q2. [SATA] The nurse practitioner is teaching nursing students about the description of atopic dermatitis (eczema). Which findings fit the classic description? Select all that apply.

A. Abnormally dry skin

B. Severe itching that gets worse in the evening

C. Lichenification in chronically scratched areas

D. Greasy, yellow, non-pruritic scale in hair-bearing areas

E. A steady course with no periods of remission once the rash appears

Show answer

Answer: A, B, C

Rationale: Eczema, "the itch that rashes," is described as abnormally dry skin with severe itching that gets worse in the evening. The itching and redness lead to dry, scaly skin, red papules, or lichenification. Greasy, non-pruritic scale describes seborrheic dermatitis. Eczema has periods of flare-ups and remission, not a steady course.

Q7. [Single] A 5-year-old with poorly controlled eczema has honey-colored crusts over excoriated, scratched areas on both forearms. The rest of the skin is unaffected. How should this impetigo be classified?

A. Primary impetigo, because the bacteria infected normal skin directly

B. Bullous impetigo, because a pre-existing skin condition is present

C. Secondary impetigo, because the infection developed at a break in the skin

D. Primary impetigo, because honey-colored crusting is present

Answer: C

Rationale: Secondary impetigo is an infection at a break in the skin, such as eczema. Primary impetigo is a direct infection of normal skin, which does not fit lesions on excoriated eczema. Honey-colored crusts are a feature of nonbullous impetigo; they do not tell you whether it is primary or secondary. Having eczema does not make impetigo bullous.

C. Rapid spread of lesions

D. A thin, varnish-like coating left after large blisters rupture

Answer: A, C, E

Rationale: Nonbullous impetigo starts as 1–2 mm thin vesicles that rupture and leave moist honey-colored crusts on red, eroded skin. It is painless but spreads quickly, and itching and regional lymphadenopathy may be present. Pain is not typical. A varnish-like coating after large blisters rupture describes bullous impetigo.

Q9. [Single] An 18-month-old has several large, flaccid blisters on the trunk. Some have ruptured and left a thin, shiny, varnish-like scale. Which type of impetigo is this, and which organism is most likely?

A. Bullous impetigo; Staphylococcus aureus

B. Nonbullous impetigo; Streptococcus pyogenes

C. Bullous impetigo; Streptococcus pyogenes

D. Nonbullous impetigo; Staphylococcus aureus

Answer: A

Rationale: In bullous impetigo, vesicles become bullae (large blisters) that rupture and leave a thin varnish-like coating or scale. Bullous impetigo is almost always caused by S. aureus. S. pyogenes can cause impetigo in general, but not typically the bullous form. Nonbullous lesions leave honey-colored crusts, not varnish-like scale.

Q10. [SATA] A nurse practitioner at a community clinic is reviewing impetigo risk factors and transmission. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.

Rationale: Impetigo is a highly contagious bacterial infection spread by contact. Risk factors are summer months, hot humid climates, and lower socioeconomic status. It is not an airborne disease, and negative-pressure isolation is not needed (compare varicella, which needs airborne precautions).

Q11. [Single] A 6-year-old with impetigo was started on appropriate therapy. After 7 days the lesions show no improvement. What is the most appropriate next diagnostic step?

A. Swab the top surface of the crust for a rapid strep test

B. Do a KOH preparation of scrapings from the lesion edge

C. Continue the same therapy for another 7 days before any testing

D. Swab beneath the crust for Gram stain, culture, and sensitivities

Show answer

Answer: D

Rationale: If there is no response after 7 days, swab beneath the crust and send a Gram stain, culture, and sensitivities to identify the organism and check for resistance. The surface of the crust is contaminated and gives poor results. KOH is used for fungal infections such as tinea. Waiting another week without testing delays the right therapy. Note: standard references say impetigo is usually diagnosed clinically, and culture is reserved for treatment failure or outbreaks.

Q14. [Single] A 7-year-old with no drug allergies has widespread impetigo that needs oral therapy. Which regimen is preferred by the professor?

A. Amoxicillin for 7–10 days

B. Dicloxacillin for 7–10 days

C. Dicloxacillin for 3 days

D. Penicillin V for 7–10 days

Show answer

Answer: B

Rationale: Oral therapy must cover beta-lactamase-producing S. aureus and is given for 7–10 days. The professor's preferred agent is dicloxacillin. Plain amoxicillin and penicillin V are broken down by beta-lactamase, so they do not reliably cover S. aureus. A 3-day course is too short. Note: standard references often list cephalexin as first-line, with dicloxacillin as an acceptable alternative.

Q18. [SATA] A 9-year-old returns from a summer camping trip in New England with an expanding red lesion on the thigh where a tick was found. Which findings are consistent with Stage 1 (early localized) Lyme disease? Select all that apply.

A. Mild nuchal rigidity

B. A rash that, without treatment, resolves permanently within 48 hours and never recurs

C. A red annular macule or papule at the bite site with a clear ring and a larger erythematous outer ring

D. Fever, malaise, and headache

E. Arthralgia and myalgia

Show answer

Answer: A, C, D, E

Rationale: Stage 1 is defined by erythema migrans (EM) at the inoculation site. It starts as a red annular macule or papule surrounded by a clear ring and then a larger erythematous outer ring, the "bull's eye." Fever, malaise, headache, arthralgia, myalgia, and mild nuchal rigidity can accompany it. Without treatment, the symptoms and rash do not go away for good; they come and go for weeks to months.

Q19. [Single] The nurse practitioner is triaging calls about tick bites. According to the blueprint, which tick bite is classified as high risk for Lyme disease transmission?

A. A flat (unengorged) tick removed about 24 hours after a hike

B. A flat tick removed within 12 hours of attachment

C. An engorged tick estimated to have fed for more than 72 hours

D. A flat tick believed to have been attached for about 30 hours

Show answer

Answer: C

Rationale: The blueprint defines a high-risk bite as an engorged tick that has fed for more than 72 hours, and a low-risk bite as a brief attachment of less than 36 hours. Options A, B, and D all fall under 36 hours, and many people remove the tick before transmission occurs. Note: standard references (AAP/IDSA) use attachment of 36 hours or more as the threshold for single-dose doxycycline prophylaxis.

Q20. [SATA] A 10-year-old had an untreated bull's-eye rash 6 weeks ago and now has signs of early disseminated (Stage 2) Lyme disease. Which findings belong to Stage 2? Select all that apply.

A. Multiple secondary annular lesions that look like the original EM but are smaller

B. Symmetric polyarthritis of the small joints of both hands

C. Carditis with first-, second-, or third-degree heart block

D. Lymphocytic meningitis

E. Radiculitis

Show answer

Answer: A, C, D, E

Rationale: Stage 2 can cause multiple smaller secondary annular lesions, lymphocytic meningitis, radiculitis, and carditis with life-threatening heart blocks. It can also cause generalized illness such as stomachache, urinary symptoms, and migratory musculoskeletal pain, and it can last weeks to 2 years without treatment. Symmetric small-joint polyarthritis of the hands is not a Lyme pattern. Lyme arthritis is a late finding that affects large joints.

Q21. [Single] Five weeks after a tick bite that was never treated, a 9-year-old suddenly develops drooping on one side of the face and cannot fully close the right eye. Which stage of Lyme disease and which cranial nerve are involved?

A. Stage 1 (early localized); cranial nerve V

B. Stage 2 (early disseminated); cranial nerve VII

C. Stage 3 (late); cranial nerve VII

D. Stage 2 (early disseminated); cranial nerve VIII

Show answer

Answer: B

Rationale: Cranial nerve palsy, specifically the facial nerve (CN VII), is a hallmark of Stage 2 early disseminated disease and appears weeks after the bite. Stage 1 is limited to EM and flu-like symptoms. Stage 3 involves arthritis and neurocognitive changes months to years later. CN V (sensation and chewing) and CN VIII (hearing and balance) do not cause facial droop with inability to close the eye.

Q22. [SATA] A 13-year-old was never treated for a probable tick bite 1 year ago and now has late (Stage 3) Lyme disease. Which findings fit this stage? Select all that apply.

A. Paresthesias of the hands and feet

Rationale: Stage 3 causes large-joint arthritis (most often the knees), cognitive and memory loss, and paresthesias of the hands and feet. Q23. [Single] A 7-year-old has fatigue, headache, and an annular rash after a summer of hiking in Pennsylvania. According to the blueprint, which diagnostic test is used for Lyme disease?

Answer: D

Rationale: The blueprint lists ELISA as the diagnostic test for Lyme disease. Western blot is a confirmatory test, not the first screen. KOH is for fungal infections. Q26. [SATA] A nurse practitioner is teaching students the difference between exanthem and enanthem findings. Which findings are classified as enanthems? Select all that apply.

A. Koplik spots

B. Pastia lines

C. Strawberry tongue

D. Slapped-cheek erythema

E. Forchheimer spots

Show answer

Answer: A, C, E

Rationale: An enanthem is an eruption on a mucous membrane, while an exanthem is an eruption on the skin. Koplik spots (oral mucosa in measles), strawberry tongue (scarlet fever), and Forchheimer spots (soft palate in rubella) are all mucosal findings. Pastia lines (skin folds) and slapped-cheek erythema (face) are skin findings, so they are exanthems.
