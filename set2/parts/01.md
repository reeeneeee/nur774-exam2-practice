**Q1. [Single]** An 8-month-old has had dry, rough, red patches on the cheeks and the outer surfaces of the arms since 3 months of age. The parent says the infant rubs her face against the crib sheets constantly, especially in the evening, and that the patches clear for a while and then come back. The father has asthma and allergic rhinitis. Which diagnosis and phase best fit this presentation?
A. Seborrheic dermatitis (cradle cap), which usually resolves in the first year
B. Atopic dermatitis, childhood phase
C. Atopic dermatitis, infantile phase
D. Irritant contact dermatitis from repeated exposure to saliva

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:** Severe itching that gets worse in the evening, dry skin, flares followed by remission, and a strong family history of atopy point to atopic dermatitis. Onset at 3 months puts it in the infantile phase (onset 1–6 months, can continue to 2 years). Seborrheic dermatitis does not itch and favors hair-bearing intertriginous areas. The childhood phase begins between 2 years and puberty.

**Q2. [SATA]** A 2-month-old has a red, scaly rash. The nurse practitioner is trying to tell atopic dermatitis apart from seborrheic dermatitis. Which findings favor atopic dermatitis? Select all that apply.
A. The infant is fussy and rubs the affected areas constantly, and it is worse in the evening
B. A symmetrical, red, scaling eruption limited to the scalp, behind the ears, and the skin folds, with no itching
C. The mother has eczema and a known elevated IgE level
D. The patches flare, clear completely, and then flare again
E. The eruption is attributed to overproduction of sebum with *Malassezia* yeast
F. Dry, rough skin with red papules on the cheeks

**Answer:** A, C, D, F
**Rationale:** Atopic dermatitis is "the itch that rashes." It causes severe itching that worsens in the evening, abnormally dry skin with red papules, periods of flare and remission, and is linked to elevated IgE with a strong family history. A non-itchy, symmetrical, scaling eruption in hair-bearing intertriginous areas, caused by sebum overproduction and *Malassezia*, describes seborrheic dermatitis.

**Q3. [Single]** A 13-year-old girl has had an itchy, dry, scaly rash in the antecubital folds for the past 3 months. She and her mother confirm she never had eczema or any other skin problem before age 13. Which phase of atopic dermatitis is this?
A. Adult phase, because onset occurred after 12 years of age
B. Childhood phase, because onset occurred before puberty was complete
C. Childhood phase, because the childhood phase covers age 2 through adolescence
D. Reactivation of the infantile phase, because adult-onset disease requires a history of infantile eczema

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:** The adult phase is defined by onset after 12 years and can continue through puberty. The childhood phase begins between 2 years and puberty, and this patient's first symptoms appeared at 13. The blueprint does not require earlier infantile disease for adult-phase onset.

B. A 3-year-old who has severe, widespread skin eruptions over the trunk and extremities at the first visit
C. A 7-year-old who is partly improved after 1 week of treatment
D. A 9-year-old whose flare began after his parents' divorce and who is improving on the treatment plan

**Answer:** A, B

Children C and E have not reached 2 weeks and are improving. Emotional stress can worsen eczema (D), but this child is responding, so referral is not needed.

**Q6. [SATA]** A 6-year-old with known atopic dermatitis comes in because "the rash looks different." Which findings suggest a secondary infection that needs evaluation and treatment beyond routine eczema care? Select all that apply.
A. Moist, honey-colored crusts over scratched areas
B. Lesions that have spread quickly over the past 2 days
C. New regional lymphadenopathy near the affected skin
D. Thickened, leathery skin in the popliteal fossae
E. Itching that is worse in the evening

**Answer:** A, B, C
**Rationale:** One treatment goal in eczema is to monitor for and prevent secondary infection. Honey-colored crusts, rapid spread, and regional lymphadenopathy suggest secondary impetigo at the broken skin. Lichenification (D) and evening itching (E) are expected features of atopic dermatitis itself.

**Q7. [Single]** A 10-year-old who has had atopic dermatitis since age 3 has thick, leathery plaques with exaggerated skin markings in both antecubital fossae. What best explains this finding?
A. A kerion caused by a delayed inflammatory reaction to a fungal infection
B. Lichenification from a long-standing itch–scratch cycle
C. Bullous impetigo that left a thin, varnish-like scale
D. Allergic contact dermatitis from a type IV delayed hypersensitivity reaction

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** In eczema, a pattern of severe itching and redness leads to dry, scaly skin. Chronic scratching thickens the skin into lichenified plaques. A kerion is a boggy, pustular scalp mass seen in tinea capitis. Varnish-like scale follows ruptured bullae. Allergic contact dermatitis causes acute erythema, vesicles, and weeping at the site of contact.

**Q8. [SATA]** A school nurse asks the nurse practitioner about the cause and spread of impetigo. Which statements match the blueprint? Select all that apply.
A. It results from bacterial colonization of the skin.
B. It is caused by *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus pyogenes*.
C. It spreads through contact and droplet contact.
D. It is caused by overgrowth of *Malassezia* yeast in hot, humid climates.
E. It spreads only through contaminated objects, not from person to person.

**Answer:** A, B, C
**Rationale:** Impetigo is a highly contagious infection caused by bacterial colonization with *S. aureus* and *S. pyogenes*. The blueprint says it spreads through contact and droplet contact. *Malassezia* causes tinea versicolor and seborrheic dermatitis, not impetigo. Impetigo clearly spreads from person to person. Note: standard references describe spread mainly by direct contact with lesions; droplet spread is not usually emphasized.

**Q9. [Single]** A 4-year-old with poorly controlled eczema develops crusted lesions over the excoriated patches on both wrists. According to the blueprint, which organism almost always causes this type of impetigo?
A. *Streptococcus pyogenes*, because the crusts are honey-colored
B. *S. aureus* and *S. pyogenes* equally, so the organism cannot be predicted
C. *Malassezia ovalis*, because the infection developed on eczematous skin
D. *Staphylococcus aureus*

**Answer:** D
**Rationale:** Lesions that form at a break in the skin, such as eczema, are secondary impetigo. The blueprint states that bullous and secondary impetigo are almost always caused by *S. aureus*. Honey-colored crusts describe the nonbullous form and do not identify the organism. *Malassezia* causes seborrheic dermatitis, not impetigo.

**Q10. [SATA]** Which of these presentations are secondary impetigo? Select all that apply.
A. Crusted lesions over excoriated eczema on the wrists
B. Honey-colored crusts at the sites of scratched mosquito bites
C. Crusted lesions forming on a healing abrasion on the knee
D. Honey-colored crusts that appeared on previously normal, intact skin around the nares in a child with no skin condition
E. Crusted, oozing lesions over scratched varicella vesicles

**Answer:** A, B, C, E
**Rationale:** Secondary impetigo is an infection at a break in the skin, such as eczema, a bite, an abrasion, or scratched varicella lesions (pyoderma is a known varicella complication). Option D is primary impetigo, which is a direct bacterial infection of normal skin.

**Q11. [Single]** A healthy 5-year-old with no drug allergies has two small, superficial, painless lesions with honey-colored crusts on the chin. No other lesions are present. Which plan is most appropriate?
A. Mupirocin twice daily, applied over the intact crusts
B. Retapamulin three times daily after removing the crusts
C. Retapamulin twice daily after removing the crusts
D. Oral dicloxacillin for 7–10 days

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:** Superficial, nonbullous impetigo limited to a small area is treated with a topical antibiotic, applied after the crusts are removed. Retapamulin is dosed twice daily and mupirocin three times daily, so options A and B each have the wrong frequency, and A also leaves the crusts on. Oral therapy is for multiple lesions, not two lesions on the chin.

**Q12. [SATA]** For which children is topical antibiotic therapy alone appropriate for impetigo? Select all that apply.
A. A 4-year-old with one superficial, nonbullous lesion at the edge of the nares
B. A 6-year-old with two small nonbullous lesions on one cheek
C. A 3-year-old with many lesions on the face, trunk, and both legs
D. A 5-week-old with extensive bullae who is feeding poorly
E. A 7-year-old with a small, superficial crusted patch on the back of one hand

**Answer:** A, B, E
**Rationale:** Topical antibiotics are for impetigo that is superficial, nonbullous, or limited to a small area, which fits children A, B, and E. Multiple widespread lesions (C) call for oral antibiotics. Severe bullous disease in an infant (D) calls for a parenteral antistaphylococcal penicillin.

**Q13. [SATA]** A 6-year-old with no drug allergies has many impetigo lesions on the arms and legs. Which oral antibiotics are acceptable choices according to the blueprint? Select all that apply.
A. Cephalexin
B. Amoxicillin/clavulanate
C. Dicloxacillin
D. Amoxicillin alone
E. Penicillin V
F. Mupirocin three times daily as the only therapy

**Answer:** A, B, C
**Rationale:** Oral therapy must work against beta-lactamase-producing *S. aureus*. The blueprint lists cephalexin, amoxicillin/clavulanate, and dicloxacillin (the professor's preference), with clindamycin reserved for penicillin allergy. Plain amoxicillin and penicillin V are broken down by beta-lactamase. Mupirocin is topical and is not enough on its own for multiple lesions.

**Q14. [Single]** A 4-year-old who attends daycare starts oral cephalexin for impetigo at 9:00 a.m. on Monday. Which plan matches the blueprint?
A. Return to daycare Tuesday at 9:00 a.m.; follow up in 48–72 hours if not improved; if there is no response by 7 days, swab beneath the crust for Gram stain, culture, and sensitivities
B. Return to daycare Wednesday at 9:00 a.m.; follow up in 48–72 hours if not improved; if there is no response by 7 days, swab beneath the crust for Gram stain, culture, and sensitivities
C. Return to daycare Tuesday at 9:00 a.m.; follow up in 7 days if not improved; if there is no response by 14 days, swab the surface of the crust for culture
D. Return to daycare Tuesday at 9:00 a.m.; follow up in 48–72 hours if not improved; if there is no response by 7 days, swab the top of the crust for a rapid strep test

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:** Children stay out of daycare until they have been treated for 24 hours, and follow-up is scheduled in 48–72 hours if there is no improvement. If there is no response after 7 days, swab beneath the crust for Gram stain, culture, and sensitivities. Option B uses 48 hours of exclusion. Option C uses the wrong follow-up timing and swabs the crust surface. Option D uses the wrong swab site and the wrong test.

**Q15. [SATA]** A well-appearing 20-month-old has several large, flaccid blisters on the trunk and thighs. Some have ruptured and left a thin, varnish-like scale. The nurse practitioner decides on oral therapy. Which parts of the plan are appropriate? Select all that apply.
A. Choose an agent that is active against beta-lactamase-producing *S. aureus*
B. Treat for 7–10 days
C. Recognize that this type of impetigo is almost always caused by *S. aureus*
D. Admit for parenteral nafcillin, because all bullous impetigo in children under 2 requires IV therapy

**Answer:** A, B, C

**Q17. [Single]** Ten days after a camping trip in Connecticut, a 9-year-old has an 8-cm round lesion on the thigh. It has a red center, a clear ring, and a larger red outer ring. The border is not scaly, and the lesion is not warm or tender. He also has fever, headache, and myalgias. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Tinea corporis
B. Cellulitis
C. Stage 1 (early localized) Lyme disease with erythema migrans
D. Stage 2 (early disseminated) Lyme disease

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:** A single bull's-eye lesion at the bite site with fever, headache, and myalgia is erythema migrans, the hallmark of Stage 1. Tinea corporis and cellulitis are on the blueprint's differential, but tinea has red, scaly borders and cellulitis is warm and tender without target rings. Stage 2 would cause multiple smaller secondary annular lesions or systemic findings such as cranial nerve palsy.

**Q18. [SATA]** A parent calls after finding a tick on her child's scalp and asks about the risk of Lyme disease. Which statements match the blueprint? Select all that apply.
A. Many people remove the tick before transmission happens.
B. A brief attachment of less than 36 hours is considered a low-risk bite.
C. An engorged tick that has fed for more than 72 hours is a high-risk bite.
D. A flat tick attached for 24 hours is high risk, because any attachment longer than 12 hours transmits infection.
E. Engorgement does not matter; only the location of the bite determines risk.

**Answer:** A, B, C
**Rationale:** The blueprint notes that many people remove the tick before transmission. It defines a low-risk bite as attachment for less than 36 hours and a high-risk bite as an engorged tick that has fed for more than 72 hours. Option D contradicts the low-risk definition, and option E ignores engorgement, which is a key part of the high-risk definition. Note: standard references (AAP/IDSA) use attachment of 36 hours or more as the threshold for single-dose doxycycline prophylaxis.

**Q19. [SATA]** A child with Lyme disease is being evaluated. Which findings show that the disease has progressed beyond Stage 1? Select all that apply.
A. Multiple annular lesions that look like the original EM but are smaller
B. Facial droop from a cranial nerve VII palsy
C. Lymphocytic meningitis
D. Mild nuchal rigidity with a single EM lesion
E. Fever and malaise with a single EM lesion

**Answer:** A, B, C
**Rationale:** Multiple smaller secondary annular lesions, CN VII palsy, and lymphocytic meningitis are all Stage 2 (early disseminated) findings. Mild nuchal rigidity, fever, and malaise are listed among the symptoms that come with the EM rash in Stage 1. Do not confuse mild nuchal rigidity with lymphocytic meningitis.

**Q20. [Single]** Two months after an untreated bull's-eye rash, a 10-year-old has several smaller annular lesions on the trunk, and a lumbar puncture confirms lymphocytic meningitis. Which additional finding belongs to the same stage of Lyme disease?
A. Arthritis of the knee with WBCs in the synovial fluid
B. Paresthesias of the hands and feet
C. Cognitive and memory loss
D. Radiculitis

**Answer:** D
**Rationale:** Secondary annular lesions and lymphocytic meningitis place this child in Stage 2 (early disseminated), which also includes radiculitis. Large-joint arthritis with synovial WBCs, paresthesias of the hands and feet, and cognitive and memory loss are all Stage 3 (late) findings. Note that Stage 2 radiculitis and Stage 3 paresthesias both involve nerves, which makes them easy to mix up.

**Q22. [Single]** A parent asks how long early disseminated (Stage 2) Lyme disease can last if it is never treated. Which answer matches the blueprint?
A. Weeks to 2 years
B. Weeks to months, with symptoms that come and go
C. It appears months to years after the original infection
D. A few weeks, then it resolves permanently

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:** Untreated, Stage 2 lasts from weeks to 2 years. Intermittent symptoms lasting weeks to months describe untreated Stage 1 (EM and its accompanying symptoms). Appearance months to years after infection describes late (Stage 3) disease. Untreated Lyme disease does not resolve permanently in a few weeks.

**Q23. [SATA]** Six weeks after an untreated bull's-eye rash, an 8-year-old comes in with a fainting episode, bradycardia, and second-degree heart block on ECG. Which statements are accurate? Select all that apply.
A. This presentation fits early disseminated (Stage 2) Lyme disease.
B. Lyme carditis can cause first-, second-, or third-degree heart block.
C. Heart block means the child has reached Stage 3 (late) disease.
D. Antibiotics given during the EM stage would have prevented progression to Stage 3.
E. Arrhythmias are not a feature of Lyme disease, so another cause should be the only focus.

**Answer:** A, B, D
**Rationale:** Carditis with life-threatening first-, second-, or third-degree heart block is a Stage 2 (early disseminated) finding. Treating with antibiotics in the early stages prevents progression to Stage 3. Stage 3 involves large-joint arthritis, cognitive changes, and paresthesias, not carditis. Arrhythmias are a well-documented Lyme complication.

**Q24. [Single]** A 12-year-old who was never treated for a tick bite 14 months ago had a swollen right knee. Fluid aspirated during the episode contained WBCs. The swelling went away on its own after 3 weeks, and the parents are relieved. What is the nurse practitioner's best response?
A. "The swelling went away on its own, which shows it was not Lyme arthritis."
B. "Untreated Lyme arthritis often goes away within a few weeks, but it tends to come back and become chronic."
C. "Since the swelling has resolved, the disease can no longer progress."
D. "Knee arthritis is a Stage 2 finding that resolves within 2 years."

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** WBCs in the synovial fluid are concrete evidence of Lyme arthritis, a Stage 3 finding that affects large joints, most often the knee. Untreated, the arthritis resolves in a few weeks but becomes recurrent and chronic, so spontaneous resolution neither rules out Lyme disease nor means it is cured. Large-joint arthritis is late (Stage 3) disease, not Stage 2.

**Q25. [Single]** A 10-year-old has a single classic EM lesion and flu-like symptoms after a tick exposure. Which regimen matches the blueprint and the professor's notes?
A. Amoxicillin for 7–10 days
B. Cefuroxime for 5–7 days
C. Doxycycline for 14–21 days
D. A single dose of doxycycline with no further therapy

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:** The blueprint lists doxycycline, amoxicillin, or cefuroxime, and the professor specified a 14–21 day course. Options A and B use acceptable drugs with courses that are too short. A single dose of doxycycline is not the treatment for an established EM rash. Note: standard references (IDSA 2020/AAP) recommend about 10 days of doxycycline or 14 days of amoxicillin or cefuroxime for EM, and reserve single-dose doxycycline for post-bite prophylaxis.
