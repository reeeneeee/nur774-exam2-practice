NUR 774 Exam 3 — 20 NCLEX-Style Practice Questions

Q1. [Single] A 2-week-old is admitted with tachypnea, temperature instability, and a right lower lobe infiltrate. The mother had prolonged rupture of membranes. Which organism is the MOST likely cause?

A. Streptococcus pneumoniae

B. Mycoplasma pneumoniae

C. Group B Streptococcus

D. Respiratory syncytial virus

Show answer

Answer: C

Rationale: In neonates (0–1 month), the leading pneumonia pathogens are acquired around delivery: Group B Streptococcus, E. coli (linked to maternal infection and premature rupture of membranes), and Listeria. S. pneumoniae is the leading bacterial cause from 1 month to 5 years and beyond. Mycoplasma is a school-age/teen pathogen. RSV is the most common cause in infants past the newborn period, especially in winter.

Q2. [SATA] A 14-year-old has had 10 days of gradually worsening low-grade fever, malaise, and a dry, persistent cough. He has kept going to school. Which organisms are MOST consistent with this presentation in his age group? Select all that apply.

A. Mycoplasma pneumoniae

B. Chlamydophila pneumoniae

C. Listeria monocytogenes

D. Streptococcus pneumoniae

E. Group B Streptococcus

Show answer

Answer: A, B, D

Rationale: In school-age children (5–18 years), atypical bacteria predominate. Mycoplasma causes "walking pneumonia," and Chlamydophila causes mild, gradual-onset symptoms. S. pneumoniae remains a significant cause at every pediatric age. Listeria and GBS are neonatal pathogens acquired from the mother.

Q3. [Single] A 6-year-old recovering from influenza suddenly develops high fever, rapid breathing, and a necrotizing pneumonia on chest x-ray. Which pathogen should the NP be MOST concerned about?

A. Staphylococcus aureus, including MRSA

B. Haemophilus influenzae type B

C. Pneumocystis jirovecii

D. Chlamydophila pneumoniae

Show answer

Answer: A

Rationale: S. aureus, including MRSA, is a classic cause of severe secondary bacterial pneumonia after influenza. Hib is rare in vaccinated children. Pneumocystis occurs in immunocompromised children, such as those with HIV. Chlamydophila causes mild, gradual-onset illness, not a sudden necrotizing pneumonia.

Q4. [SATA] Which congenital heart defects are classified as ACYANOTIC? Select all that apply.

A. Coarctation of the aorta

B. Patent ductus arteriosus

C. Tricuspid atresia

D. Pulmonary stenosis

E. Transposition of the great arteries

F. Atrial septal defect

Show answer

Answer: A, B, D, F

Rationale: Acyanotic defects either increase pulmonary blood flow through a left-to-right shunt (VSD, ASD, PDA) or obstruct flow without cyanosis (aortic stenosis, pulmonary stenosis, coarctation). Tricuspid atresia and transposition are cyanotic defects. They send deoxygenated blood to the body or limit blood flow to the lungs.

Q5. [Single] Which group of findings makes up tetralogy of Fallot?

A. ASD, pulmonary stenosis, left ventricular hypertrophy, overriding aorta

B. VSD, aortic stenosis, right ventricular hypertrophy, overriding aorta

C. VSD, pulmonary stenosis, right ventricular hypertrophy, overriding aorta

D. VSD, pulmonary stenosis, left ventricular hypertrophy, coarctation of the aorta

Show answer

Answer: C

Rationale: The four defects in TOF are VSD, pulmonary stenosis, right ventricular hypertrophy, and an overriding aorta. Each distractor swaps one element: ASD for VSD, aortic for pulmonary stenosis, left for right ventricular hypertrophy, or coarctation for overriding aorta. The RV hypertrophies because it pumps against the narrowed pulmonary outflow.

Q6. [SATA] A 3-month-old has a loud, harsh holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border with a palpable thrill. Which other findings would the NP expect if the defect is large? Select all that apply.

A. Sweating and tiring during feeds

B. Poor weight gain

C. Recurrent respiratory infections

D. Fixed split S2

E. Persistent central cyanosis at rest

Show answer

Answer: A, B, C

Rationale: A holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border with a thrill indicates a VSD. A large VSD causes heart failure from increased pulmonary blood flow, seen as diaphoresis and fatigue with feeds, failure to thrive, and recurrent respiratory infections or pneumonia. A fixed split S2 is the hallmark of ASD. VSD is acyanotic, so cyanosis at rest is not expected unless complications develop.

Q7. [Single] A 5-year-old's well-child exam reveals a grade II/VI systolic ejection murmur at the upper left sternal border with a widely split S2 that does NOT change with respiration. Which defect is MOST likely?

A. Ventricular septal defect

B. Atrial septal defect

C. Patent ductus arteriosus

D. Coarctation of the aorta

Show answer

Answer: B

Rationale: ASD causes a systolic ejection murmur from increased flow across the pulmonary valve and a fixed split S2. ASDs are often asymptomatic in childhood. A VSD causes a holosystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border. A PDA causes a continuous "machinery" murmur. Coarctation causes differences in BP or pulses between the arms and legs.

Q8. [SATA] A 7-year-old has had sore throat for 1 day. Which findings support GABHS pharyngitis rather than a viral cause? Select all that apply.

A. Petechiae on the soft palate

B. Sudden onset of sore throat with fever of 39°C

C. Cough and rhinorrhea

D. Tender, swollen anterior cervical lymph nodes

E. Headache and abdominal pain with vomiting

F. Hoarseness and conjunctivitis

Show answer

Answer: A, B, D, E

Rationale: Strep throat presents with sudden onset, fever, palatal petechiae, tender cervical nodes, and often headache, nausea, or vomiting in children. Cough, rhinorrhea, hoarseness, and conjunctivitis point to a viral cause. The absence of cough and rhinorrhea is a key distinguishing feature of GABHS.

Q9. [Single] Three weeks after an untreated sore throat, a 9-year-old has fever, migratory pain in the knees and ankles, a new murmur, and pink rings with raised edges on the trunk. Which complication is MOST likely?

A. Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis

B. PANDAS

C. Kawasaki disease

D. Acute rheumatic fever

Show answer

Answer: D

Rationale: Migratory polyarthritis, carditis (new murmur), and erythema marginatum are major Jones criteria for acute rheumatic fever. ARF is a nonsuppurative complication that develops weeks after untreated GABHS pharyngitis. PSGN presents with cola-colored urine, facial edema, and hypertension. PANDAS presents with sudden OCD symptoms or tics. Kawasaki disease affects children under 5 and is not tied to strep.

Q10. [SATA] Which complications of GABHS infection are SUPPURATIVE? Select all that apply.

A. Peritonsillar abscess

B. Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis

C. Retropharyngeal abscess

D. Sydenham chorea

E. Otitis media

F. Necrotizing fasciitis

Show answer

Answer: A, C, E, F

Rationale: Suppurative complications come from direct spread of the bacteria: peritonsillar abscess, retropharyngeal abscess, otitis media, sinusitis, and necrotizing fasciitis. PSGN and Sydenham chorea (part of rheumatic fever) are nonsuppurative, immune-mediated complications. They occur weeks later without direct infection of the tissue.

Q11. [Single] An 11-year-old with asthma has symptoms 4 days a week, but not daily. She wakes at night 3 times a month, uses her SABA 4 days a week, and has minor limitation in gym class. Her FEV1 is 85% predicted. How should her asthma be classified?

A. Intermittent

B. Mild persistent

C. Moderate persistent

D. Severe persistent

Show answer

Answer: B

Rationale: Mild persistent asthma means symptoms more than 2 days a week but not daily, nighttime awakenings 3–4 times a month, SABA use more than 2 days a week but not daily, minor activity limitation, and FEV1 over 80%. Intermittent requires everything at 2 days a week or less and awakenings 2 or fewer times a month. Moderate persistent requires daily symptoms and an FEV1 of 60–80%.

Q12. [SATA] Which findings would classify a child's asthma as MODERATE persistent? Select all that apply.

A. Daily symptoms

B. Nighttime awakenings more than once a week but not nightly

C. Daily SABA use

D. FEV1 between 60% and 80% of predicted

E. SABA use several times a day

F. FEV1 less than 60% of predicted

Show answer

Answer: A, B, C, D

Rationale: Moderate persistent asthma is defined by daily symptoms, awakenings more than once a week but not nightly, daily SABA use, some activity limitation, and FEV1 of 60–80%. SABA use several times a day and FEV1 below 60% define severe persistent asthma. Remember the classification is based on the most severe category any single feature falls into.

Q13. [Single] During which stage of pertussis is the patient MOST contagious?

A. Catarrhal stage

B. Paroxysmal stage

C. Convalescent stage

D. Contagiousness is equal across all three stages

Show answer

Answer: A

Rationale: The catarrhal stage (1–2 weeks) looks like a common cold, with runny nose, mild cough, sneezing, and low-grade fever. This is when pertussis is most contagious, and it is hard to recognize. The paroxysmal stage (1–6 weeks) brings the classic coughing fits with an inspiratory whoop and post-tussive vomiting. The convalescent stage is the gradual recovery over weeks to months.

Q14. [SATA] A 3-month-old with pertussis is in the paroxysmal stage. Which complications is this infant at highest risk for? Select all that apply.

A. Apnea

B. Pneumonia

C. Seizures

D. Rib fractures

E. Weight loss from post-tussive vomiting

F. Urinary incontinence

Show answer

Answer: A, B, C, E

Rationale: Infants face the most severe complications: apnea, pneumonia, seizures, weight loss from vomiting and feeding difficulty, and rarely hypoxic brain damage. Rib fractures and urinary incontinence from forceful coughing are complications typical of adolescents and adults. Infants also often do not "whoop"; apnea or cyanosis may be the presenting sign.

Q15. [Single] A previously healthy 2-year-old had a sudden choking and coughing episode while eating peanuts an hour ago. He now has a persistent cough and wheezing heard only over the right lung, with decreased breath sounds on that side. There is no fever. Which diagnosis is MOST likely?

A. Croup

B. Asthma exacerbation

C. Bronchiolitis

D. Foreign body aspiration

Show answer

Answer: D

Rationale: A sudden choking episode with no fever or viral prodrome, followed by unilateral wheezing and decreased breath sounds, is classic for foreign body aspiration. The right mainstem bronchus is the most common site. Asthma and bronchiolitis cause diffuse, bilateral wheezing. Croup causes a barking cough and inspiratory stridor after a cold-like prodrome.

Q16. [SATA] An 18-month-old is brought in at 2 a.m. after 2 days of runny nose. Which findings are consistent with croup? Select all that apply.

A. Barking, seal-like cough

B. Inspiratory stridor that worsens with crying

C. Hoarse voice

D. Low-grade fever around 38°C

E. Drooling, sitting forward, and refusing to swallow

F. High fever with a toxic appearance

Show answer

Answer: A, B, C, D

Rationale: Croup, most often from parainfluenza, affects children 6 months to 3 years. It causes a barking cough, inspiratory stridor (worse with agitation and at night), hoarseness, and low-grade fever after a cold-like prodrome. Drooling, tripod positioning, high fever, and a toxic appearance suggest epiglottitis or bacterial tracheitis, which are airway emergencies.

Q17. [Single] A 2-year-old with croup has stridor AT REST, frequent barking cough, and mild retractions. Her oxygen saturation is normal. How is this severity classified, and what is the key medication?

A. Mild; humidified air only

B. Moderate; oral dexamethasone

C. Moderate; nebulized epinephrine as the first-line home treatment

D. Severe; humidified air only

Show answer

Answer: B

Rationale: Moderate croup is frequent barking cough, stridor at rest, and mild respiratory distress. Corticosteroids such as dexamethasone reduce airway inflammation in moderate to severe croup. Mild croup has no stridor at rest. Severe croup has persistent stridor, significant distress, and signs of hypoxia. Nebulized epinephrine is given in the hospital or ED for severe cases, not as home treatment.

Q18. [SATA] A 15-month-old has fever and has been pulling at the left ear after a cold. Which otoscopic findings support a diagnosis of acute otitis media? Select all that apply.

A. Bulging tympanic membrane

B. Opaque, cloudy tympanic membrane

C. Decreased mobility on pneumatic otoscopy

D. Translucent, pearly gray membrane with normal mobility

E. Red tympanic membrane

Show answer

Answer: A, B, C, E

Rationale: AOM causes a red, opaque, bulging TM with reduced or absent mobility on pneumatic otoscopy. Bulging is the hallmark finding. A translucent, pearly gray TM that moves normally is a normal exam. Redness alone can come from crying or fever, which is why bulging and decreased mobility carry the most weight.

Q19. [Single] Which murmur is MOST consistent with an innocent (functional) murmur in an asymptomatic, thriving 4-year-old?

A. Grade II/VI low-pitched, vibratory, musical systolic murmur at the left lower sternal border

B. Grade IV/VI harsh holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border with a thrill

C. Grade II/VI diastolic murmur at the left upper sternal border

D. Grade III/VI systolic murmur with a click and fatigue on exertion

Show answer

Answer: A

Rationale: Still's murmur is the most common innocent murmur in children. It is a soft (grade I–II), low-pitched, vibratory or musical systolic murmur at the left lower sternal border in a child with normal growth and no symptoms. A thrill (grade IV or higher) or harsh holosystolic quality suggests a VSD. Diastolic murmurs are usually pathologic. Clicks and exertional symptoms also point to heart disease.

Q20. [SATA] A 3-year-old has had 6 days of fever, bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis, a rash, and an enlarged cervical lymph node. Which statements correctly distinguish this condition from rheumatic fever? Select all that apply.

A. The major complication is coronary artery aneurysm rather than valvular heart disease.

B. It is treated with IVIG and aspirin.

C. It is diagnosed with the Jones criteria and evidence of a prior strep infection.

D. It primarily affects children under 5 years old.

E. It requires long-term antibiotic prophylaxis to prevent recurrence.

Show answer

Answer: A, B, D

Rationale: This is Kawasaki disease: prolonged fever, conjunctivitis, rash, and cervical lymphadenopathy in a child under 5. It is a medium-vessel vasculitis treated with IVIG and aspirin, and the feared complication is coronary artery aneurysm. Rheumatic fever (ages 5–15) follows GABHS infection, is diagnosed with the Jones criteria, causes valvular heart disease, and needs long-term antibiotic prophylaxis.
